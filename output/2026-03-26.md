# Foundational Concepts

## Moments, Estimators, and the Bias-Variance Decomposition

For a random variable $X$ with distribution $P$, the $k$-th raw moment is $\mu'_k = \mathbb{E}[X^k]$ and the $k$-th central moment is $\mu_k = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^k]$, where $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$. The sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ is the canonical unbiased estimator of $\mu$: by the strong law of large numbers, $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu$.

The sample variance $S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2$ uses Bessel's correction ($n-1$ rather than $n$) to yield an unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X)$. The uncorrected MLE $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2$ has bias $-\sigma^2/n$, which matters in small-sample regimes common in few-shot learning.

**Example:** The **bias-variance decomposition** of MSE for an estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta$ is:

$$\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] = \underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta\right)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}])^2\right]}_{\text{Variance}}$$

This generalizes to the generalization error of ML models: the expected test loss decomposes into irreducible noise $\sigma^2_\epsilon$, squared bias from model misspecification, and variance from sensitivity to the finite training set. Regularization (L1/L2, dropout, early stopping) explicitly trades increased bias for reduced variance.

**Why it matters:** Self-distillation iterates this tradeoff — the teacher's predictions act as soft labels that reduce output variance across training runs, but systematic teacher errors accumulate as bias in the student across generations. Understanding the decomposition is essential for diagnosing whether a model's performance gap is a capacity problem (high bias) or a data problem (high variance).

## Measure-Theoretic Probability and Conditional Expectation

A probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ consists of a sample space $\Omega$, a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$ of measurable events, and a probability measure $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ satisfying countable additivity. This formalism is essential for reasoning rigorously about infinite-dimensional function spaces — the setting of most modern deep learning theory.

Conditional probability is defined for $P(B) > 0$ as $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B) / P(B)$. Events $A, B$ are independent iff $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$, which is strictly stronger than zero correlation (uncorrelated does not imply independent except under Gaussianity).

**Example:** **Conditional expectation** $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ for a sub-$\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ is the unique $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable satisfying:

$$\int_G \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \, dP = \int_G X \, dP \quad \forall G \in \mathcal{G}$$

This is the Radon-Nikodym derivative $d(P_X|_\mathcal{G}) / dP|_\mathcal{G}$. In practice, $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X=x]$ is the regression function — the optimal predictor of $Y$ from $X$ under squared loss. Bayes' theorem follows directly: $p(\theta \mid x) \propto p(x \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)$, and the posterior concentrates around the MLE as $n \to \infty$ (Bernstein-von Mises theorem).

**Why it matters:** The policy gradient theorem for RL — central to today's GUI agent papers — relies on this machinery. The gradient of the expected return is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[R(\tau)] = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\!\left[R(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau)\right]$$

The log-derivative (score function) trick exchanges differentiation and expectation under the integral sign via the dominated convergence theorem — a result that only holds given the measure-theoretic structure above.
